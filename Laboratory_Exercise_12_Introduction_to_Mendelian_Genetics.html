11  Introduction to Mendelian Genetics

11.1 Introduction

Gregor Mendel is widely recognized as the father of modern genetics because his experiments provided the first clear explanation of how inherited characteristics are transmitted from one generation to the next. Mendel was born in 1822 and grew up on a farm, where he developed an early interest in plants. His later education in mathematics and science, especially at the University of Vienna, helped him apply quantitative reasoning to biological questions.

Between 1856 and 1865, Mendel conducted controlled breeding experiments using pea plants (Pisum sativum). He selected pea plants because they possessed easily observed traits, reproduced quickly, and could be artificially fertilized under controlled conditions. By tracking characteristics such as seed shape, seed color, flower color, and plant height across generations, he demonstrated that traits are inherited as discrete hereditary units rather than blending together. These findings became the conceptual basis for what are now called genes.

Mendel presented his results to the Natural History Society of Brno in 1865 and published them in 1866 in a paper titled Experiments in Plant Hybridization. Despite the importance of his conclusions, the work attracted little attention at the time. Historians and science references note that his paper circulated only narrowly and that many contemporary scientists did not appreciate the significance of his mathematical treatment of inheritance. Mendel’s work was not widely recognized until about 1900, when other scientists rediscovered similar inheritance patterns and connected them to his earlier publication.

The modern language of genetics emerged after Mendel, but the principles he discovered remain foundational. A gene is a unit of heredity, and a trait is a heritable characteristic influenced by one or more genes. Alternative versions of a gene are called alleles, and the specific physical position of a gene on a chromosome is its locus. Diploid organisms inherit two alleles for most genes, one from each parent. When both alleles are the same, the organism is homozygous for that gene; when the alleles differ, the organism is heterozygous.

The outward expression of a genetic characteristic is the phenotype, whereas the underlying allele combination is the genotype. In classical Mendelian inheritance, a dominant allele is expressed in the phenotype whenever it is present, while a recessive allele is expressed only when two recessive copies are inherited. However, not all inheritance patterns follow simple dominance. In incomplete dominance, the heterozygous phenotype is intermediate between the two homozygous phenotypes. In codominance, both alleles are fully expressed in the heterozygote. In sex-linked inheritance, the gene responsible for a trait is located on a sex chromosome, most often the X chromosome.

In this laboratory, students will first analyze a monohybrid cross, which follows the inheritance of a single trait. Monohybrid crosses are especially useful for examining complete dominance, incomplete dominance, codominance, and sex-linked traits. Students will then examine a dihybrid cross, which tracks two traits simultaneously and illustrates how genes may assort independently during gamete formation. Together, these exercises introduce the predictive framework of Mendelian genetics.

Objectives

Upon completion of this laboratory exercise, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the historical significance of Gregor Mendel’s work in establishing the foundations of modern genetics.

  2. Define essential genetic terms, including gene, trait, locus, allele, genotype, phenotype, homozygous, heterozygous, dominant, and recessive.

  3. Distinguish between monohybrid and dihybrid crosses and explain the biological questions each type of cross addresses.

  4. Predict genotypic and phenotypic ratios from monohybrid crosses involving complete dominance.

  5. Interpret inheritance patterns that deviate from simple dominance, including codominance, incomplete dominance, and sex-linked inheritance.

  6. Use Punnett square analysis to evaluate expected outcomes of genetic crosses.

  7. Relate observed inheritance patterns to Mendel’s principles of segregation and independent assortment.

11.3 Monohybrid Cross Problems Using a Punnett Square

A monohybrid cross follows the inheritance of a single gene or trait. In a Punnett square, the possible gametes from one parent are written across the top, and the possible gametes from the other parent are written along the side. Each box inside the square represents one possible offspring genotype. This allows students to predict both genotypic ratios and phenotypic ratios for a cross.

For a trait that follows complete dominance, the dominant allele is expressed whenever it is present, while the recessive allele is expressed only when an individual has two recessive copies.

Problem 1: Worked Example: Homozygous Dominant × Homozygous Recessive

In pea plants, purple flowers (P) are dominant to white flowers (p*). Cross a homozygous dominant purple-flowered plant with a homozygous recessive white-flowered plant.

  • Parent 1 genotype = PP

  • Parent 2 genotype = p*p*

Step 1. Determine the gametes from each parent

Because each gamete receives only one allele for the gene, the PP parent can produce only P gametes, and the p*p* parent can produce only p* gametes. This follows Mendel’s principle of segregation.

  • Gametes from PP: _____, _____

  • Gametes from p*p*: _____, _____

Step 2. Set up the Punnett square

Fill in the outer boxes below

Step 3. Identify the offspring genotypes

All four boxes contain the genotype Pp*.

  • Offspring genotypes: 100% Pp*

  • Genotypic ratio: 0 PP : 4 Pp* : 0 p*p*

  • Simplified genotypic ratio: 100% heterozygous

Step 4. Identify the offspring phenotypes

Because P is dominant, every offspring with genotype Pp* will have purple flowers. In complete dominance, a heterozygote shows the dominant phenotype.

  • Offspring phenotypes: 100% purple flowers

  • Phenotypic ratio: 4 purple : 0 white

Conclusion

A cross between a homozygous dominant parent and a homozygous recessive parent produces offspring that are:

  • 100% heterozygous

  • 100% dominant phenotype

This is a useful first example because every offspring has the same genotype and the same phenotype.

How to Solve Any Monohybrid Cross

You can use the following method for every problem:

  1. Write the genotypes of both parents.

  2. Determine the possible gametes from each parent. Each gamete carries only one allele for the trait.

  3. Draw a 2 × 2 Punnett square for a monohybrid cross.

  1. Place one allele from each parent in one of the outside boxes.

  2. Fill in the four internal boxes by combining one allele from each parent.

  3. Count the genotypes to determine the genotypic ratio.

  4. Use dominance rules to determine the phenotypic ratio.

11.4 Extensions of Monohybrid Crosses

Not all inherited traits follow the simple dominant–recessive pattern first described by Mendel in pea plants. Although Mendel’s work established the basic principles of segregation and inheritance, later studies demonstrated that some traits exhibit alternative inheritance patterns, including codominance, incomplete dominance, and sex-linked inheritance. These patterns can still be analyzed using the same general approach as a standard monohybrid cross, but the relationship between genotype and phenotype differs from that seen in complete dominance. [Experiment...gor Mendel], [en.wikipedia.org]

In the following sections, you will examine three important extensions of Mendelian inheritance. For each pattern, you should determine the parental genotypes, identify the possible gametes, construct a Punnett square, and then predict both the genotypic and phenotypic outcomes of the cross. Although the Punnett square method remains the same, interpretation of the offspring changes depending on the dominance relationship between the alleles or on whether the gene is carried on an autosome or a sex chromosome.

11.4.1 Monohybrid Crosses with Codominance

In codominance, both alleles in a heterozygous individual are fully and distinctly expressed in the phenotype; one allele does not mask the other. A classic biological example is the ABO blood group system, in which the Iᴬ and Iᴮ alleles are codominant and are both expressed in individuals with genotype IᴬIᴮ, producing blood type AB. Allele i, when it appears, is recessive and is masked by either IA or IB. In laboratory exercises, codominance is often illustrated with coat-color traits in animals, such as red and white coat alleles in cattle, where the heterozygote displays both red and white hairs.

Solved Example: Codominance

In cattle, red coat color (Cᴿ) and white coat color (Cᵂ) are codominant. A heterozygous animal with genotype CᴿCᵂ has a roan coat, meaning that both red and white hairs are visible. Cross two roan cattle.

Parental genotypes:

  • Parent 1: CᴿCᵂ

  • Parent 2: CᴿCᵂ

Possible gametes:

  • Each parent produces Cᴿ and Cᵂ gametes.

              Cᴿ        Cᵂ
    Cᴿ   CᴿCᴿ    CᴿCᵂ
    Cᵂ   CᴿCᵂ    CᵂCᵂ

Offspring genotypes:

  • 1 CᴿCᴿ

  • 2 CᴿCᵂ

  • 1 CᵂCᵂ

Genotypic ratio: 1 : 2 : 1

Offspring phenotypes:

  • CᴿCᴿ = red

  • CᴿCᵂ = roan

  • CᵂCᵂ = white

Phenotypic ratio: 1 red : 2 roan : 1 white

This example illustrates that, in codominance, the heterozygote has a phenotype in which both alleles are visibly expressed, rather than one allele masking the other or producing an intermediate blend.

11.4.2 Monohybrid Crosses with Sex Linkage

A sex-linked trait is determined by a gene located on a sex chromosome, most commonly the X chromosome. In humans and many other organisms, females typically have two X chromosomes (XX) and males have one X and one Y chromosome (XY). Because males have only one X chromosome, they possess only a single copy of most X-linked genes and will express a recessive X-linked allele whenever it is present. Females generally must inherit two recessive alleles to express a recessive X-linked trait. As a result, X-linked recessive conditions often occur more frequently in males than in females.

A common instructional example is red–green color blindness, which is usually treated as an X-linked recessive trait. In this notation, Xᴺ represents the normal-vision allele and Xⁿ represents the color-blind allele; the Y chromosome does not carry a corresponding allele for this trait.

11.4.3 Monohybrid Crosses with Incomplete Dominance

In incomplete dominance, neither allele is completely dominant over the other, and the heterozygote displays an intermediate phenotype between the two homozygotes. This pattern differs from codominance because the heterozygote does not show both traits distinctly; instead, it shows a phenotype that appears blended or intermediate. A classic example is snapdragon flower color, in which red-flowered plants and white-flowered plants produce pink-flowered heterozygous offspring.

A commonly used notation for this trait is:

  • CᴿCᴿ = red

  • CᵂCᵂ = white

  • CᴿCᵂ = pink

Solved Example: Incomplete Dominance

In snapdragons, flower color shows incomplete dominance. Cross two pink flowers.

Parental genotypes:

  • Parent 1: CᴿCᵂ

  • Parent 2: CᴿCᵂ

  • Each parent produces ______ and ______ gametes.

Complete the Punnett Square below.

  
———————– ———————– ———————–

Offspring genotypes:

  • ____ CᴿCᴿ

  • ____ CᴿCᵂ

  • ____ CᵂCᵂ

Offspring phenotypes:

  • CᴿCᴿ = red

  • CᴿCᵂ = pink

  • CᵂCᵂ = white

Phenotypic ratio: 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white

This example shows that in incomplete dominance the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype, and therefore the phenotypic ratio matches the genotypic ratio in a heterozygote × heterozygote cross.

11.4.4 Comparative Summary

These three types of monohybrid crosses illustrate that not all single-gene traits follow complete dominance. In codominance, both alleles are fully expressed in the heterozygote. In incomplete dominance, neither allele is fully dominant, and the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype. In sex-linked inheritance, the pattern of transmission depends on whether the gene is located on a sex chromosome and whether the offspring is male or female. Despite these differences, each pattern can still be analyzed using the same stepwise process of identifying parental genotypes, determining gametes, constructing a Punnett square, and interpreting the resulting genotypic and phenotypic ratios., https://courses.lumenlearning.com/suny-oneonta-osbiology2e-1/chapter/characteristics-and-traits/

11.5 Dihybrid Crosses

A dihybrid cross is a genetic cross that examines the inheritance of two different traits (two genes) simultaneously. While a monohybrid cross follows one trait, a dihybrid cross allows us to observe how alleles of different genes are inherited together and whether they assort independently. This concept reflects Mendel’s principle of independent assortment, which states that alleles of different genes are distributed independently into gametes (provided the genes are not linked).

In a dihybrid cross, each parent contributes two alleles for each of two genes, and each gamete receives one allele from each gene. As a result, more combinations of gametes are possible compared to a monohybrid cross.

Using FOIL to Determine Gametes

To solve dihybrid crosses efficiently, students can use the FOIL method, a technique adapted from algebra:

  • First

  • Outer

  • Inner

  • Last

This method helps determine all possible gamete combinations from a heterozygous individual.

Example:

For a genotype AaBb, apply FOIL:

  • First: A B → AB

  • Outer: A b → Ab*

  • Inner: a B → a*B

  • Last: a b → a*b*

Thus, this individual produces four types of gametes:

  • AB, Ab*, a*B, a*b*

Summary

Dihybrid crosses extend the principles learned in monohybrid crosses by examining two traits simultaneously. The use of the FOIL method simplifies the process of determining gametes, and Punnett squares allow prediction of offspring outcomes. When both parents are heterozygous and genes assort independently, the expected phenotypic ratio is 9:3:3:1, but deviations from this ratio can reveal additional biological insights such as linkage or test crosses.

11.6 Key Terms / Glossary

Trait – A heritable characteristic or feature of an organism, such as flower color or seed shape.

Gene – A basic unit of heredity that contributes to a specific biological characteristic.

Locus – The specific location of a gene on a chromosome.

Allele – One of two or more alternative versions of a gene at a given locus.

Homozygous – Having two identical alleles for a gene.

Heterozygous – Having two different alleles for a gene.

Dominant – Refers to an allele that is expressed in the phenotype when present in a heterozygous individual.

Recessive – Refers to an allele whose effect is masked in a heterozygous individual and expressed only when two recessive alleles are present.

Phenotype – The observable physical or physiological characteristics of an organism.

Genotype – The allele combination or genetic constitution of an organism.

Monohybrid Cross – A genetic cross that tracks the inheritance of a single trait or gene.

Dihybrid Cross – A genetic cross that tracks the inheritance of two different traits or genes at the same time.

Complete Dominance – A pattern of inheritance in which the heterozygote has the same phenotype as the homozygous dominant individual.

Incomplete Dominance – A pattern of inheritance in which the heterozygous phenotype is intermediate between the two homozygous phenotypes.

Codominance – A pattern of inheritance in which both alleles are fully expressed in the heterozygous phenotype.

Sex-Linked Inheritance – Inheritance of traits determined by genes located on the sex chromosomes, commonly the X chromosome.

  3. Why is it important to distinguish between phenotype and genotype when analyzing a genetic cross?

  4. In a monohybrid cross involving complete dominance, why can two organisms with the same phenotype have different genotypes?

  5. What genotypic ratio is expected when two heterozygous individuals are crossed for a trait with complete dominance?

  6. What phenotypic ratio is expected in the same cross?

  7. How does the phenotypic ratio in incomplete dominance differ from the phenotypic ratio in complete dominance for a heterozygote × heterozygote cross?

  8. How does codominance differ conceptually from incomplete dominance?

  9. Why do sex-linked traits often appear more frequently in one sex than in the other?

  10. What additional information can be learned from a dihybrid cross that cannot be learned from a monohybrid cross?

  11. Explain how a dihybrid cross can be used to illustrate the principle of independent assortment.

  12. If observed data from a cross do not exactly match expected ratios, what biological or experimental factors might explain the discrepancy?

  13. Which type of inheritance pattern in this lab did you find most conceptually challenging, and why?

  14. How does understanding patterns of inheritance help biologists interpret heredity in plants, animals, and humans?

11.8 Procedure Overview

Part I. Review of Mendelian Terminology and Genetic Notation

  1. Review the definitions of the terms provided in the glossary.

  2. Practice writing genotypes using standard letter notation for dominant and recessive alleles.

  3. Distinguish between genotype and phenotype for several sample organisms or traits.

Part II. Monohybrid Crosses with Complete Dominance

  1. Examine a genetic trait controlled by two alleles in which one allele is completely dominant to the other.

  2. Construct Punnett squares for parental crosses provided by the instructor or laboratory handout.

  3. Determine the expected genotypic and phenotypic ratios for the offspring.

  4. Compare predicted ratios with any observed class or sample data, if provided.

Part III. Monohybrid Crosses with Incomplete Dominance

  1. Analyze a trait in which the heterozygous phenotype is intermediate between the two homozygous phenotypes.

  2. Use Punnett square analysis to predict offspring genotypes and phenotypes.

  3. Record expected ratios and compare them with complete dominance outcomes.

Part IV. Monohybrid Crosses with Codominance

  1. Examine a trait in which both alleles are expressed in the heterozygous condition.

  2. Complete Punnett square analysis for the assigned codominant cross.

  3. Compare the resulting phenotypic ratios with those seen in complete and incomplete dominance.

Part V. Monohybrid Crosses with Sex Linkage

  1. Review the inheritance of a trait controlled by a gene located on the X chromosome.

  2. Use sex-linked notation to represent parental genotypes.

  3. Predict the expected phenotypic distribution among male and female offspring.

  4. Identify why reciprocal crosses may produce different results in sex-linked inheritance.

Part VI. Dihybrid Crosses

  1. Construct Punnett squares or use a forked-line method to analyze a cross involving two separately inherited traits.

  2. Determine the expected genotypic and phenotypic ratios.

  3. Evaluate how the results illustrate Mendel’s principle of independent assortment.

Part VII. Interpretation and Discussion

  1. Summarize the inheritance pattern observed in each type of cross.

  2. Compare different modes of inheritance and identify how they alter expected phenotypic ratios.

  3. Answer post-laboratory questions using both calculations and written explanations.
