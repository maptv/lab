3.2 Introduction: Molecules, Water, and pH

Biology is the study of life, and understanding life begins with understanding the chemistry that makes it possible. In this first-semester biology lab, you will explore the fundamental chemical principles that underlie biological systems, preparing you for advanced work as a biology major or pre-med student. Through hands-on experiments and model-based activities, you will connect basic chemical concepts to real biological function.

Chemical compounds can be divided into two broad categories: inorganic and organic. Inorganic compounds, such as water, salts, acids, and bases, generally do not contain carbon–hydrogen (C–H) bonds and play essential roles in maintaining life processes. Organic molecules, on the other hand, are defined by the presence of carbon and C–H bonds. Carbon’s unique ability to form four covalent bonds allows it to create a wide variety of complex molecules, especially when combined with other key elements such as oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur—elements that make up the vast majority of living matter.

In this lab, you will investigate how atoms form molecules through covalent bonding, where electrons are shared between atoms. You will learn how to represent molecules using molecular formulas, structural formulas, and abbreviated structural formulas, each providing different levels of detail. Using molecular model kits, you will build three-dimensional representations of molecules to better understand how atomic arrangement influences molecular shape and function. You will also explore isomers, which are molecules with the same molecular formula but different structures, demonstrating how small differences in arrangement can lead to significant biological differences.

A major focus of this lab is water, one of the most important inorganic molecules for life. Because of its polar structure, water exhibits unique properties such as cohesion, adhesion, high surface tension, and high specific heat. These properties allow water to act as a solvent, regulate temperature, and move through narrow spaces via capillary action—all essential for sustaining life. You will perform experiments to observe and measure these properties directly.

You will also examine acids, bases, and pH, which are critical for maintaining proper conditions in biological systems. The pH scale measures the concentration of hydrogen ions in a solution and determines whether a solution is acidic, neutral, or basic. Because the pH scale is logarithmic, even small changes represent significant shifts in chemical conditions. Living systems depend on tightly regulated pH levels—for example, human blood must remain within a narrow range to support proper enzyme and cellular function. To maintain this balance, organisms rely on buffers, which resist changes in pH when acids or bases are introduced.

By combining theory with experimentation, this lab will help you develop essential scientific skills, including observation, data collection, analysis, and critical thinking. These foundational concepts will support your future studies in biology, chemistry, and medicine, and provide insight into how chemical processes sustain life at every level.

3.3 Activity 1: Lab Experiment

Watch the video on the link here to understand the activity we will be performing.

Objectives:

By the end of this laboratory exercise, students should be able to:

  1. Describe how the addition of acids and bases affects the pH of a solution.

  2. Demonstrate proper use of a pH probe and computer interface to measure pH.

  3. Accurately collect, record, and organize experimental data.

  4. Construct and interpret graphs showing pH changes in response to added substances.

  5. Compare pH changes in plain water versus buffered solutions.

  6. Explain how buffers resist changes in pH when acids or bases are added.

  7. Analyze experimental results to identify patterns and draw conclusions.

  8. Relate the concept of buffering to biological systems and homeostasis.

Materials

  • 1 laptop computer

  • 1 computer interface

  • 2 pH probes

  • 2 microstirrers (rinse with deionized water before use)

  • Stir plate setup (stir plate, rod, and clamp)

  • 1 – 250 mL beaker (for rinsing probes)

  • 2 – 50 mL beakers (rinse before each use)

  • 1 – 100 mL graduated cylinder (rinse before use)

  • ~125 mL tap water (for probe storage)

  • 1 rinse bottle with distilled/deionized water

  • 0.10 M HCl (acid) with dropper

  • 0.10 M NaOH (base) with dropper

  • 2 Bufferin tablets

General Setup

  1. Put on your safety goggles.

  2. Add 40 mL of distilled water to each of two 50 mL beakers.

  3. Label the beakers:

    • A (Acid)

    • B (Base)

  4. Place both beakers on stir plates.

Preparing the pH Probes

  1. Remove each pH probe from its storage container.

  2. Rinse the probe tip thoroughly:

    • Hold it over a 250 mL beaker

    • Use distilled water to rinse

Important:

  • Do not let the probe dry out

  • Keep it in tap water when not in use

  • The tip is fragile glass—handle carefully

Computer Setup

  1. Set up the computer and interface (follow your instructor’s guide).

  2. Plug the probes into:

    • CH1

    • CH2

  3. Open the “Bio 110” folder on the desktop.

  4. Open “Acids Bases Template.”

You should see both probes listed on the screen. If not, check connections or try a different USB port.

Part 1: Testing Acids and Bases in Water

  1. Place the probes into the beakers:

    • CH1 probe → Beaker A (acid)

    • CH2 probe → Beaker B (base)

  2. Turn on the stir plates.

  3. Click “COLLECT” to begin recording pH.

  4. Wait until the pH readings stabilize.

  5. Click “KEEP,” enter 0 (no drops yet), and click OK.

Adding Acid and Base

    • 5 drops of acid to Beaker A

    • 5 drops of base to Beaker B

  2. Wait for the readings to stabilize.

  3. Click “KEEP” and enter the total number of drops added.

  4. Repeat steps 6–8 until you reach 30 total drops.

  5. Click “STOP.”

3.4 Activity 2: Demonstration of Capillary Action (Adhesion and Cohesion)

In this exercise, you will observe how water can move from one container to another without direct pouring. This activity demonstrates the properties of water such as cohesion and adhesion, which allow water to travel through materials like paper towels. Over time, you will record your observations to better understand how water moves through capillary action.

  1. Take two 250 ml beakers and put them side by side.

  2. Fill one with water. Keep the second empty.

  3. Take a piece of paper towel and fold it lengthwise in the middle a few times to get a thin strip. Fold it in the middle to make a V shape.

  4. Dip one end of the paper towel strip into the empty beaker and the other end into the beaker full of water. Allow this to sit until the end of the lab period. Check the beakers every 60 mins and make observations. Record your observations below.

3.5 Activity 3: Demonstration of Surface Tension (Adhesion and Cohesion)

In this exercise, you will investigate the property of surface tension in water. Surface tension is caused by hydrogen bonding between water molecules, creating a thin “skin” at the surface. By observing how a paperclip floats and how detergent affects this behavior, you will better understand how intermolecular forces influence the properties of water.

  1. Fill a culture dish halfway with water. Float a small paperclip on the surface of the water. This requires careful placement of the paperclips to avoid breaking the surface tension of the water.

  2. Use a dropper to carefully deposit a drop of water near the floating paperclips. Record your observations.

  3. Next, deposit several drops of liquid detergent near the floating paperclips. After applying the detergent, try to float another paperclip. The detergent disrupts the hydrogen bonds in water. Record your observations below.

3.6 Activity 4: Building Models

In this activity, you will explore how molecules are represented using molecular, structural, and abbreviated structural formulas. A molecular formula, such as glycine (C₂H₅NO₂), shows the number and types of atoms present, while structural formulas display how those atoms are bonded. Abbreviated structural formulas simplify these representations by grouping atoms, making complex molecules easier to visualize.

You will use a molecular model kit to build three-dimensional representations of molecules, helping you understand how atoms connect through covalent bonds. As you construct models, you will also observe how the same molecular formula can form different structures, known as isomers. Additionally, you will examine how chemical reactions involve breaking and forming covalent bonds to create new products.

3.8 Instructor Ancillaries

Pre-Lab Support

  • Provide a short mini-lecture (~10–15 min) on:

    • Bonding and molecular representation

    • Water properties

    • pH basics

  • Use visual models or slides for:

    • Molecular vs structural formulas

    • Polarity diagrams

  • Assign pre-lab quiz for completion grade

  • Encourage students to review key terms beforehand

During Lab

  • Demonstrate:

    • Floating paperclip technique

    • Proper model kit use

    • pH probe/calibration (if applicable)

  • Circulate and check:

    • Model accuracy

    • Data recording

  • Prompt critical thinking with questions like:

    • “What do you think will happen next?”

    • “Why did that change occur?”

Post-Lab Support

  • Facilitate discussion:

    • Compare group results

    • Address misconceptions

  • Provide answer key or rubric

  • Connect to real-world applications:

    • Medicine (blood pH)

    • Environmental science (acid rain)

Assessment Suggestions

  • Pre-lab quiz (graded)

  • Lab worksheet completion

  • Post-lab reflection or report

  • Concept quiz (short answer emphasis)

Common Student Challenges

  • Confusing molecular vs structural formulas

  • Difficulty visualizing 3D structures

  • Misunderstanding pH scale (logarithmic nature)

  • Misinterpreting cause of surface tension changes

Tips for Success

  • Use hands-on demonstrations

  • Reinforce connections to human physiology (for pre-med students)

  • Encourage group collaboration

  • Provide guided questions rather than answers
