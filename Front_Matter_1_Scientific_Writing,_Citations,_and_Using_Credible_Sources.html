Scientific Writing, Citations, and Using Credible Sources

  2. Why must credible sources be used in biology?

Overview

Biology is a scientific discipline grounded in observation, experimentation, and evidence-based reasoning. Scientific knowledge does not exist in isolation; it builds upon the work of other researchers. As a result, scientists must evaluate credible sources, cite prior work properly, and communicate findings clearly and accurately.

In this course, you will learn how to:

  • Ask scientific, testable questions

  • Identify and evaluate credible biological sources

  • Properly cite scientific literature

  • Communicate findings using accepted formats of scientific writing

Together, these skills form the foundation of scientific literacy and integrity.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students will be able to:

  1. Distinguish between scientific and non-scientific questions

  2. Identify credible sources appropriate for biological research

  3. Differentiate between primary, secondary, popular, and scholarly sources

  4. Evaluate source credibility using objective criteria (CRAAP test)

  5. Explain the importance of citation in scientific writing

  6. Define plagiarism and identify examples of academic dishonesty

  7. Apply proper in-text citation formats (APA or CSE style)

  8. Describe the structure and purpose of sections in a scientific paper

  9. Distinguish between Results and Discussion sections

  10. Use writing practices that improve clarity, accuracy, and credibility

Scientific Questions and the Role of Evidence

Science focuses on questions that can be tested through observation, measurement, and experimentation.

Scientific Questions

  • Can be investigated empirically

  • Rely on measurable data

  • Are testable and repeatable

Examples:

  • What temperature maximizes bacterial growth?

  • Does a specific drug reduce disease symptoms?

Non-Scientific Questions

  • Cannot be tested using scientific methods

  • Often involve opinions, beliefs, or ethics

Examples:

  • What is the best genre of music?

  • Should cloning be allowed?

Only scientific questions can be addressed in laboratory investigations.

Using Credible Sources in Biology

Before conducting research, scientists review existing knowledge. Using credible sources ensures accuracy and reliability.

Types of Scientific Sources

Primary Sources

  • Original research articles

  • Present new data and experimental findings

  • Peer-reviewed and written by experts

Secondary Sources

  • Summarize or interpret primary research

  • Examples: textbooks, review articles

Popular vs. Scholarly Sources

The following table compares popular and scholarly sources, highlighting key differences in audience, review process, and reliability in scientific research.

Table 1. Comparison of Popular and Scholarly Sources in Scientific Research

This table compares popular and scholarly sources across several characteristics. Popular sources are written for a general audience, are not peer-reviewed, contain few or no citations, use simplified language, and include examples such as blogs and magazines. Scholarly sources are written for an expert audience, are peer-reviewed, include extensive references, use technical language, and include examples such as scientific journals like Science and Nature.

Popular Sources Scholarly Sources
General audience Expert audience
Not peer-reviewed Peer-reviewed
Few or no citations Extensive references
Simplified language Technical language
Examples: blogs, magazines Examples: Science, Nature

Scientific writing relies primarily on scholarly sources.

Evaluating Credibility: The CRAAP Test

To assess sources, use the CRAAP criteria:

  • Currency – Is the information recent?

  • Reliability – Is it from a trusted organization or journal?

  • Authority – Is the author qualified?

  • Accuracy – Is it supported by evidence?

  • Purpose – Is the content objective or biased?

Low-quality sources should not be used in scientific work.

Citing Sources and Avoiding Plagiarism

Why Citation Matters

Citations:

  • Distinguish your ideas from others

  • Credit original authors

  • Allow verification of information

  • Maintain academic integrity

What Is Plagiarism?

Plagiarism is presenting another person’s work as your own.

Examples:

  • Copying text without citation

  • Paraphrasing without credit

  • Submitting someone else’s work

Avoiding Plagiarism

Plagiarism is the act of presenting another person’s work, ideas, words, or creative output as your own without giving proper credit to the original source. This can include copying text directly, paraphrasing someone else’s ideas without acknowledgment, or submitting work that was created by someone else, such as a classmate or an online source. Plagiarism is considered a serious academic and ethical violation because it misrepresents authorship, undermines intellectual honesty, and deprives the original creator of recognition for their work.

  • Use your own words

  • Cite all non-original ideas

  • Keep accurate research notes

  • Use quotes sparingly in science

Citation Styles in Science

Scientific disciplines use standardized formats such as:

  • APA (Author–Year)

  • CSE (Name–Year commonly used in biology)

In-Text Citation Example

  • (Smith, 2020)

Reference List

  • Includes only sources cited in the paper

  • Organized alphabetically

Consistency in formatting is essential.

Writing for Science

Scientific writing emphasizes clarity, precision, and conciseness to ensure that ideas and findings are communicated effectively. Clarity means presenting information in a straightforward and understandable manner, avoiding ambiguity or unnecessary complexity. Precision involves using specific, accurate language and terminology so that the meaning is exact and scientifically correct. Conciseness requires eliminating unnecessary words or repetition, allowing the reader to quickly grasp the essential information without distraction. Together, these principles help make scientific communication efficient, credible, and accessible to other researchers.

Structure of a Scientific Paper

The structure of a scientific paper provides a standardized format that allows researchers to communicate findings clearly and logically. Each section has a specific purpose: the Introduction presents background and the research question, the Methods describe how the study was conducted, the Results report the data, and the Discussion interprets the findings. This organized approach helps readers quickly understand the study and evaluate its validity.

Title

  • Concise and descriptive

Abstract

  • Summary of purpose, methods, results, and conclusions

  • Written last

Introduction

  • Provides background and context

  • States research question or hypothesis

  • Explains importance of the study

Methods

  • Describes how the study was conducted

  • Allows replication

  • Includes materials and procedures

Results

  • Presents data and findings

  • Includes figures and tables

  • No interpretation

Discussion

  • Interprets results

  • Connects findings to existing research

  • Explains significance and limitations

Literature Cited

  • Lists all referenced sources

General Writing Practices

  • Be concise and precise

  • Use appropriate scientific terminology

  • Prefer active voice when appropriate

  • Use past tense for results, present tense for general knowledge

  • Avoid ambiguity and unnecessary detail

  • Treat “data” as plural

  • Italicize scientific names (Homo sapiens)

  • Revise and proofread carefully

Summary and Application to Laboratory Work

This chapter introduces the foundational skills required for success in biology, emphasizing the importance of scientific thinking, credible research, proper citation, and effective communication. Biology relies on evidence-based investigation, meaning that students must be able to ask testable scientific questions, evaluate reliable sources, and interpret data accurately. Understanding how to distinguish between scientific and non-scientific questions ensures that laboratory work remains focused on measurable and reproducible outcomes.

A key component of scientific work is the use of credible sources, particularly scholarly and peer-reviewed materials. Students learn to differentiate between primary and secondary sources and to evaluate credibility using criteria such as the CRAAP test. This ensures that conclusions are based on accurate, unbiased, and scientifically valid information.

Equally important is the ethical use of information. Proper citation practices allow students to give credit to original researchers, avoid plagiarism, and strengthen the credibility of their work. Using consistent citation formats such as APA or CSE helps maintain professional standards in scientific communication.

The chapter also introduces scientific writing, which prioritizes clarity, precision, and conciseness. Students learn the standard structure of a scientific paper—including Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion—and the purpose of each section. This structure ensures that experiments can be clearly understood, evaluated, and replicated.

Application to a Pre-Lab or Laboratory Report

In a pre-lab, students apply these skills by:

  • Formulating testable scientific questions or hypotheses

  • Researching background information using credible sources

  • Properly citing references used to justify the experiment

In a laboratory report, students:

  • Use the Introduction to explain the scientific question and support it with cited research

  • Describe procedures clearly in the Methods to allow replication

  • Present data objectively in the Results section

  • Interpret findings in the Discussion, connecting results to scientific literature

  • Include a properly formatted Literature Cited section

Overall, these skills ensure that laboratory work is not only accurate and ethical but also aligned with the standards of the scientific community.

Appendix: Scientific Writing, Citations, and Credible Sources Terms

Term Definition
Abstract A brief summary of a scientific paper including purpose, methods, results, and conclusions.
Accuracy The correctness of information, supported by evidence and reliable sources.
APA Style A citation format that uses author and date for referencing sources.
Authority The credibility and qualifications of an author or source.
Bias A tendency to present information in a non-objective or unfair way.
Citation A reference to a source used to give credit and allow verification.
Clarity The quality of being easy to understand in writing.
Conciseness Expressing ideas clearly using as few words as necessary.
CRAAP Test A tool used to evaluate sources based on Currency, Reliability, Authority, Accuracy, and Purpose.
Credible Source A reliable and trustworthy source of information supported by evidence and expertise.
CSE Style A citation format commonly used in biology that emphasizes author and year.
Discussion A section of a scientific paper that interprets results and explains their significance.
Empirical Based on observation or experiment rather than theory alone.
Introduction The section of a paper that provides background and states the research question or hypothesis.
Literature Cited A list of all sources referenced in a scientific paper.
Methods The section describing how an experiment was conducted.
Peer Review The evaluation of scientific work by experts before publication.
Plagiarism Using another person’s work or ideas without giving proper credit.
Precision Using exact and accurate language in scientific writing.
Primary Source An original research article presenting new data.
Purpose The intended goal or objective of a source or study.
Reliability The consistency and dependability of a source.
Results The section that presents data without interpretation.
Scholarly Source A peer-reviewed source written for a scientific audience.
Scientific Question A question that can be tested through observation and experimentation.
Secondary Source A source that summarizes or interprets primary research.
Significance The importance or meaning of findings in a scientific context.
Source Evaluation The process of determining the credibility of a source.
Testable Able to be examined through scientific experiment or observation.
