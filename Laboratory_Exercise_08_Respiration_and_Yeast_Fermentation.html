8  Respiration and Yeast Fermentation

8.1 Introduction: Respiration and Yeast Fermentation

All living organisms need energy to survive. Plants and animals take energy from the environment and convert it into forms their cells can use. In plants, this begins with photosynthesis, where light energy from the sun is converted into chemical energy stored in glucose. This stored energy then becomes available to living cells through cellular respiration, a series of metabolic pathways that break down glucose and convert its energy into ATP, the usable form of energy for cellular work.

Sugars such as glucose are especially important because they have a large amount of energy stored in their chemical bonds. In organisms that use oxygen, cellular respiration is essentially the reverse of photosynthesis. In this process, glucose reacts with oxygen to produce carbon dioxide, water, and energy:

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O

During aerobic respiration, oxygen serves as the final electron acceptor in the electron transport chain, allowing cells to produce a large amount of ATP using energy carried by molecules such as NADH and FADH₂.

But when oxygen is unavailable, cells must use alternative pathways to keep producing energy. In these anaerobic conditions, cells rely on fermentation to regenerate NAD⁺ from NADH so that glycolysis can continue producing ATP. Fermentation uses organic molecules as final electron acceptors and does not require oxygen.

There are two common types of fermentation. Lactic acid fermentation occurs in animal muscle cells during intense exercise and in certain bacteria; it converts pyruvate into lactic acid while regenerating NAD⁺:

C₆H₁₂O₆ → Pyruvic acid + NADH ↔︎ lactic acid + NAD⁺

Alcohol fermentation, used by yeast, converts pyruvate into ethanol and carbon dioxide while also regenerating NAD⁺:

C₆H₁₂O₆ → Pyruvic acid → CO₂ + acetaldehyde → ethanol

Yeast are single-celled eukaryotic fungi that are widely used in industry to produce bread, beer, wine, and various chemicals. In this lab, you will investigate how yeast carry out alcohol fermentation and produce carbon dioxide (CO₂) while breaking down sugars for energy.

During cellular respiration, yeast metabolize sugars and release CO₂ as a byproduct. You will test three monosaccharides—glucose, fructose, and galactose—which all share the same chemical formula (C₆H₁₂O₆) but differ in structure as isomers.

Glucose Galactose Fructose

You will also examine two disaccharides, lactose and sucrose, which have the same chemical formula (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) but different molecular arrangements.

Sucrose Lactose Maltose

By comparing how yeast ferments these different sugars, you will see how molecular structure influences the rate and efficiency of fermentation. Overall, this experiment will help you understand how cells generate energy under anaerobic conditions through processes such as fermentation.

8.3 Procedures

  1. Effects of Substrate on Respiration

Procedure:

  1. Gather 6 beakers and 6 screw-cap test tubes. Label for each set: Water, Glucose, Fructose, Galactose, Lactose, Maltose, and Sucrose.

  2. Add 1 g of yeast to each beaker. Then add 20 mL of the matching solution to each labeled beaker. Mix thoroughly.

  3. Fill the corresponding test tube completely with the yeast mixture. Secure the cap, then carefully invert the tube into the beaker. (Extra liquid will remain in the beaker.)

  4. Leave the setup on your lab bench. As fermentation occurs, CO₂ gas will form, pushing liquid out of the tube and creating an air space.

  5. When the CO₂ reaches the first marked line on the test tube, begin recording the volume of gas every 5 minutes for 60 minutes. Record your data in the table.

  6. If the tube fills with CO₂ before 60 minutes, calculate the hourly rate using: (Total mL CO₂÷time in minutes) ×60.

  7. Record your data in table x.x, then plot the data on the accompanying graph.

  1. Effects of Temperature on Respiration

You will repeat the experiment to test how temperature affects the rate of respiration. Set up five more beakers and test tubes, but this time use only one sugar, glucose. This part of the lab will help you understand how temperature changes influence yeast respiration.

  1. Set up 5 new beakers and 5 test tubes. In this part, you will use glucose only as the sugar source.

  2. Add 20 mL of glucose solution to each beaker.

  3. Before adding yeast, place the beakers in their assigned water bath or incubator for at least 5 minutes, so the solution reaches the correct temperature.

  4. Once the solution has adjusted to the temperature, add 1 g of yeast to each beaker and mix well.

  5. Fill a test tube completely with the mixture, cap it, and invert it into the beaker. Then return the setup to the correct temperature environment.

  6. Allow at least 20 minutes for the water baths to reach the correct temperature before starting.

  7. Measure the amount of CO₂ produced every 5 minutes for 60 minutes and record your data in the results table.

8.4 Appendix: Respiration and Yeast Fermentation Terms

Term Definition
Aerobic Respiration The process of breaking down glucose using oxygen to produce ATP, carbon dioxide, and water.
Alcohol Fermentation An anaerobic process that converts pyruvate into ethanol and CO2.
Anaerobic A process that occurs without oxygen.
ATP Adenosine triphosphate, the main energy currency of the cell.
Carbon Dioxide (CO2) A gas produced as a byproduct of cellular respiration and fermentation.
Cellular Respiration A series of metabolic reactions that convert glucose into ATP.
Electron Transport Chain A series of proteins that transfer electrons and generate ATP during respiration.
Ethanol Alcohol produced during yeast fermentation.
Fermentation An anaerobic process that allows ATP production by regenerating NAD+ from NADH.
Fructose A monosaccharide sugar that can be used by yeast for fermentation.
Galactose A monosaccharide that must be converted before it can enter glycolysis.
Glucose A primary sugar used by cells to produce energy.
Glycolysis The first step of cellular respiration where glucose is broken into pyruvate.
Isomer Molecules with the same chemical formula but different structures.
Lactic Acid Fermentation An anaerobic process that converts pyruvate into lactic acid.
Lactose A disaccharide composed of glucose and galactose.
Maltose A disaccharide consisting of two glucose molecules.
Monosaccharide A single sugar molecule such as glucose or fructose.
NAD+ An electron carrier required for glycolysis to continue.
NADH A reduced electron carrier that donates electrons in respiration.
Oxygen A gas used as the final electron acceptor in aerobic respiration.
Pyruvate A three-carbon molecule produced during glycolysis.
Reaction Rate The speed at which a biochemical reaction occurs.
Respiration The process by which cells harvest energy from glucose.
Substrate A substance that is metabolized or acted upon in a reaction.
Sucrose A disaccharide composed of glucose and fructose.
Temperature A factor that affects enzyme activity and reaction rate.
Yeast A unicellular fungus used as a model organism for fermentation.
