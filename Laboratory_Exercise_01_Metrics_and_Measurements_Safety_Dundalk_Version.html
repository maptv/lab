Overall Laboratory Unit Objectives

1.1 Introduction: Laboratory Safety and Measurement

Welcome to your first biology lab! Before you begin conducting experiments, it is essential to learn how to work safely and accurately in a laboratory setting. This lab will introduce you to the basic skills and habits that scientists use every day.

In the first part of this lab, you will learn about laboratory safety. Even in an introductory biology lab, there are potential hazards such as chemicals, glassware, biological materials, and heat sources. Understanding how to recognize these risks—and how to respond to them—helps protecting you, your classmates, and your experiments. Following safety rules also ensure that your results are reliable and free from contamination. These safety practices are not only important in class but are also essential in real-world careers such as healthcare, research, and biotechnology.

In the second part of this lab, you will focus on scientific measurement, which is a key skill in all areas of science. You will learn how to use the metric (SI) system, the standard system used by scientists worldwide. You will practice measuring length, mass, and volume using common laboratory tools such as rulers, balances, graduated cylinders, and pipettes. You will also learn how to convert between units, record data correctly, and organize your results.

Finally, you will explore how scientists evaluate data by learning the difference between accuracy (how close a measurement is to the true value) and precision (how consistent repeated measurements are). You will also practice calculating percent error and displaying your data using graphs.

Together, these skills—working safely, measuring carefully, and recording data accurately—form the foundation for all future lab work. Mastering them now will help you succeed in this course and prepare you for more advanced scientific investigations.

Introduction to Laboratory Safety

Purpose

Laboratory safety is essential for protecting yourself, your classmates, and your experiments. Even in introductory biology labs, hazards such as chemicals, glassware, biological materials, and heat sources are present.

Why This Matters (Lab and Workplace)

  • Prevents injuries such as burns, cuts, and exposure to harmful materials

  • Ensures accurate experimental results (no contamination or errors)

  • Builds professional habits required in healthcare, research, and laboratory jobs

Example

If you contaminate a microbial sample by touching it without gloves, results become invalid and unusable. In a workplace lab, this could lead to incorrect diagnoses or product failure.

Section 1.1: Common Laboratory Hazards

Introduction

Even basic biology labs contain hazards that can cause injury if proper precautions are not followed.

Types of Hazards

  • Chemicals (toxins, irritants)

  • Sharp objects (glassware, pipettes)

  • Biological materials (specimens, fluids)

  • Heat and electrical equipment

Examples

  • Breaking a glass slide can cut your hand

  • Hot plates can cause burns if touched incorrectly

  • Preserved specimens may contain irritating chemicals

Section 1.2: Proper Clothing and Personal Preparation

Introduction

Clothing and preparation protect you before accidents occur.

Required Clothing

  • Closed-toe, closed-heel shoes

  • Long pants covering legs

  • Shirt covering torso and shoulders

  • Hair tied back

Not Allowed

  • Sandals, flip-flops, open shoes

  • Shorts or exposed legs

  • Loose sleeves or dangling items

Examples

  • A chemical spill on exposed skin can cause irritation or burns

  • Loose hair may catch fire or fall into chemicals

Section 1.3

Introduction

PPE is your direct barrier against hazards.

Required PPE

  • Safety goggles (ANSI-approved)

  • Gloves (when handling biological or chemical materials)

  • Lab coat (recommended)

Examples

  • Goggles prevent chemicals from entering eyes

  • Gloves prevent exposure to biological materials

Section 1.4: Safe Lab Behavior

Introduction

Safe behavior reduces accidents for everyone in the lab.

Rules

  • No eating or drinking

  • No running or horseplay

  • Keep hands away from face

  • Treat all materials as potentially hazardous

Examples

  • Eating in the lab could expose you to chemicals

  • Running could cause spills or broken glass

Section 1.5: Emergency Procedures

Introduction

Knowing what to do immediately can prevent severe injury.

Chemical Splash (Face with Goggles)

  • Do NOT remove goggles

  • Go to eyewash station immediately

  • Flush for at least 15 minutes

  • Notify instructor

Chemical Splash (Eyes)

  • Use eyewash immediately

  • Hold eyes open

  • Flush for at least 15 minutes

Examples

  • Rapid flushing can prevent permanent eye damage

  • Delaying response increases injury severity

Section 1.6: Waste Disposal and Cleanup

Introduction

Proper disposal protects people and the environment.

Guidelines

  • Dispose of biohazard materials properly

  • Use broken glass containers

  • Never leave spills unattended

  • Clean lab benches before leaving

Example

Improperly discarded glass can injure custodial staff.

Section 1. 7: Hand Washing and Hygiene

Introduction

Hygiene prevents contamination and illness.

Key Rule

  • Wash hands for at least 30 seconds before leaving lab

Example

Background:

Scientists use scientific methods to understand the natural world. An important aspect of the scientific process is collecting and analyzing data from observations and experiments. Two methods of measuring and recording data have been commonly used - the Imperial and the Metric System. The Imperial System uses units such as inches and pounds while the Metric System, a decimal-based system, uses units such as meters, liters, and grams. Because of its ease of use, the metric system is more widely used within the modern scientific community. The universal “language” of modern science is based on the metric system and learning to use it is the main purpose of this lab.

Part 1: Introduction to Metric System

Work conducted in science laboratories requires a functional understanding of the metric system, which serves as the standard system of measurement in scientific research. The metric system employs standardized, decimal-based units to measure length, mass, and volume, ensuring that measurements are precise, reproducible, and universally interpretable. Appropriate laboratory instruments are selected based on the quantity being measured; for example, balances are used to measure mass in grams, and graduated cylinders are used to measure liquid volume in milliliters.

The metric system is built upon a set of base units commonly used in biology. The meter (m) is the standard unit for measuring length; the liter (L) is used to measure volume, and the gram (g) is used to measure mass. Temperature in biological laboratories is typically measured in degrees Celsius (°C). All recorded measurements must include both a numerical value and the proper unit. For example, a measurement of 2 m indicates a length that is twice the base unit of length, the meter. Standard unit symbols and abbreviations are allowed and should be used consistently when recording data.

A primary advantage of the metric system is the ease with which units can be converted. Because the system is based on powers of ten, conversions involve multiplication or division by 10 or multiples of 10. For example, one decameter is equal to ten meters, and one kilometer is equal to one thousand meters. This base ten structure simplifies calculations, reduces the likelihood of errors, and makes the metric system especially well-suited for scientific measurements and laboratory applications.

Base Units of Measure

  • Length: meter (m)

  • Mass: gram g)

  • Volume: liter (L)

  • Time: seconds (s)

  • Temperature: degree Celsius (oC)

Reading a Meniscus

When measuring liquid volume in a pipette or graduated cylinder, it is important to read the meniscus, the curved surface of the liquid. Most liquids (like water) form a concave meniscus, meaning the surface curves downward.

To obtain a correct measurement:

  • Position your eyes level with the liquid surface to avoid parallax errors.

  • Read the measurement at the lowest point of the meniscus (the bottom of the curve) for those liquids that form a concave meniscus. For liquids that form a convex meniscus (such as mercury), the measurement should be read at the top of the curve.

  • Ensure the instrument is upright on a flat surface, and the liquid is still.

Careful alignment and consistent technique are essential for precise volume measurements.

Lab Activity 1.7: Accuracy and Precision

When making measurements, both precision and accuracy are extremely important. Precision of a measurement refers to the closeness of repeated measurements or the reproducibility of the measurement. Precision can be affected by the measuring instrument. If a student uses a thermometer calibrated to the nearest degree to measure the temperature of a beaker of water and a second student uses a thermometer that is calibrated to the nearest ten degrees for the same exercise, the reproducibility or precision of measurements will be greater for the student using the thermometer calibrated to the nearest degree. The thermometer with the greatest number of calibrations (smallest increments) is the most precise, and thus the reproducibility is greater. Accuracy, described as how close a measurement is to its true value, however, is dependent upon both the precision of the measuring instrument and the technical skills of the individual taking the measurement. You will perform an experiment to determine which instrument is most accurate and precise for measuring 10ml of liquid.
